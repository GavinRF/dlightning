From the outside this looks like a scatter. A design studio site. A blog. Eight decision tools. Three browser games. Two product apps. A book, a set of Figma templates, an AI intake portal, a payment spine.

They live under one domain and mostly in one repository, which is a decision rather than an accident. A studio that sells product validation has to be able to show the thing working on itself. So the pieces are not a portfolio of unrelated projects. They are one acquisition and delivery system, and the surfaces that look least commercial are carrying the most weight.

The loop that makes it a system

A list of properties is not a system. What turns this into one is a single path that a stranger can walk end to end without meeting a person until the moment they want to.

01ArriveA free decision tool, a game, or an essay
02KeepA branded result the visitor exports and takes
03CaptureAn email address, into a Google Form
04QualifyThe intake agent runs the discovery conversation
05RecordA lead in Firestore, reviewed in the admin console
06ConvertCheckout, webhook, entitlement, Pro tier

Step four is where the design decision lives for anyone building with agents. The intake portal replaces the first discovery call. It is deliberately unauthenticated, because asking a prospect to create an account before they have told you anything is a tax on the person least invested in paying it. Every payment endpoint on the other side of the system stays auth-guarded. Same product, opposite defaults, and the difference is a judgment about who is being asked to trust whom.

Step six is the only place the system moves money on its own. A webhook reconciles the subscription lifecycle into an entitlement record in Firestore, and that record is what the product checks when it decides whether to open the Pro tier.

The two builders feed each other

Experience Builder and Screen Builder look like two products and behave like two halves of one.

Experience Builder is where a project starts: research, personas, the shape of what is being validated. What comes out of it is intent, and intent has to become something a person can look at and react to. That is Screen Builder, where the interface gets assembled from a component library rather than drawn from scratch. The screens that come back out of it land in the Experience Builder record as the artifact the client actually responds to, which produces the next round of intent.

Neither one is interesting alone. A research tool with no output is a form. A mockup tool with no brief is a toy. The loop between them is the product, and it is the reason both are on the map with arrows in two directions.

The games are the argument

The games are the part of this map most likely to be read as a hobby, and they are the part I would defend hardest.

Oasea
You guide rainwater down into an aquifer and bring a barren island back. The mechanic is the thesis. Water is not a resource you spend down, it is a system you restore, and the win condition is a place that supports life again.
BonsaiBox
Grow and prune a living 3D tree in a zen garden. You shape it by taking away rather than adding, and the result is a form you arrived at instead of one you specified. That is most of what design actually is.
Startup Cards
A business simulator where sustainability and ethics are real cards with real costs. Open-source sustainability tooling, a carbon calculator embedded in the product, sustainable packaging, a supply chain audit that confirms suppliers meet the standards you claim, formal guidelines governing how your AI gets built. Each one costs runway now and pays back later, which is exactly the argument the studio makes to clients.

Two things follow from that. The first is that the games carry the same content as the essays and the practice page, in a form someone will spend fifteen minutes with voluntarily. Nobody reads a values statement. People will grow a tree.

The second is commercial. The games page says it plainly: these are proof of what we can build, and if you want one, tell us the problem. A studio that claims it can design an experience is more persuasive when there are three finished ones you can play in a browser with no download and no account. That is why the map draws a line from the games straight into the contact form and not into a portfolio page.

The through-line is restraint

Look at the map again and one temperament runs through the parts that have nothing to do with selling anything.

BonsaiBox is a zen garden and carries 型木 in its title. Oasea asks you to restore a water system rather than spend one down. Three of the essays are Fifteen Stones, on the Kyoto garden built around the one stone a visitor is never allowed to see, Instant Bonsai, on working with something living that remembers every decision you make, and The Psychology of "Enough", on products that do not know the word.

The book is called Your Cup of Tea. The styleguide that governs every essay carries a rule named the peer test: would a senior designer say this to a colleague over a cup of tea, or does it sound like a LinkedIn post. The instruction under it is four words. Aim for the tea.

None of that is decoration. The practice page defines autonomy as the ability to stop, to leave, and to go elsewhere with what belongs to you, and it rules out manufactured urgency and obstructed exits by name. Restraint is the house style and it is also the commercial position. The tools hand over a result and stop. The games run in a browser tab you can close.

What it all sits on

Four things underneath everything else. Firebase carries auth, data, functions and hosting for every surface that needs a server. The Collective is who does the work. The authoring pipeline is how the writing gets made without drifting, and it has its own case study covering where authority sits inside it.

The fourth is the practice, and it is the only one that explains the shape of the rest. Empathy, autonomy and unity, with autonomy defined as the user's right to leave. A studio that writes that down and then builds something designed to hold you would be lying. So the tools hand over a result and stop, the games ask for no account and no download, and the blog is more often about soil and craft than about software.

That is the part a map can almost show. The rest is in the boxes themselves, and every one of them is a click away.
