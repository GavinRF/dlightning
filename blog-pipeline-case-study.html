Teaching a Machine to Sound Human

A publishing system where the hard engineering was defining what "good" meant.

Case Study  
August 2026
Back to home

The last post of 2024 went up on October 10. The next one went up on May 10, seven months later.

Nothing had gone wrong with the ideas. I had a text file full of them. What I didn't have was the two or three hours it takes to turn an idea into something publishable, and I didn't have them repeatedly, on a schedule, for months. So the blog sat there with six posts on it, and the six posts were good, which somehow made it worse.

I built a pipeline to fix the cadence. What I ended up building was something else.

The actual problem

Writing the essay is maybe 40% of the work. The rest is the tail: find a photograph that isn't stock, credit the photographer, write an excerpt that's a hook rather than a summary, add a metadata entry, regenerate the static page, rebuild the RSS feed, add a sitemap entry, then write a version for LinkedIn that stands on its own. Every one of those is small. Together they're the reason a finished draft can sit unpublished for three weeks.

There was a second problem underneath it. Writing in bursts means every post recalibrates from scratch. You come back after two months and you're a slightly different writer. Voice drift is invisible in any single post and obvious across a year of them.

And there was a constraint that ruled out the obvious fix. The audience is mid-to-senior practitioners. For that reader, generic AI prose costs more credibility than silence does. A dormant blog says I've been busy. A blog full of slop says something I can't take back.

What I got wrong first

My first attempt was a good prompt. Write a blog post in this voice, here are three examples, go.

The output was fine. That was the problem. Fine prose has a texture to it, and once you've seen it you can't stop seeing it: every sentence the same length, an image followed immediately by a sentence explaining the image, three examples where two would do, an ending that resolves into uplift. I could feel what was wrong before I could name any of it.

The realization that reorganized the whole project: generation is the cheap part. The judgment is the product.

Any model can produce 800 words on a theme. What it can't do is know that "quiet" is overused, or that opening every post with a sensory scene turns a voice into a genre. That knowledge lived in my head as taste. To hand off the drafting, I had to write the taste down.

So the system I built is mostly constraints. The prompt is a small part of it.

Inspiration is a lens, never a source

A folder of transcripts feeds thematic input. The rule on them is absolute: never quote, never mirror an argument's structure, never reference the speaker. The test I wrote for myself is a deletion test. If the transcript vanished, would the post still stand on its own?

Ideas are seeds, not headlines

A running backlog, unchecked for unused and checked for published, currently 44 lines with 23 still open. One rule here matters more than the rest: the idea is not the title. Titles have to surface from the finished writing. If a title could have been written before the post existed, it's the wrong title.

The agent drafts and stops

This is the load-bearing part. The drafting agent writes two files to a drafts folder and is explicitly forbidden from touching live content, the build script, or git. Approval is given fully to the human in the loop.

The build is boring on purpose

A single metadata JSON is the source of truth. A Python script renders it through a template into static pages and regenerates the RSS feed from the same data, so the feed can't go stale. Two details took a rewrite to get right: the generator diffs rendered output and only writes files that actually changed, and the sidebars render client-side from the same JSON. Before that, publishing one post rewrote all 33 pages and produced a diff I couldn't review. Now it's a one-file change.

Distribution is part of the pipeline

Each promoted post gets a native social version that gives the idea away completely rather than teasing it, plus a separate first comment holding the link. A queue file sequences them so two systems pieces don't run back to back.

The constraint layer

This is the part that took the longest and the part I'd hand to someone else first. A sample of what's in it, and why:

Banned words carry their reasoning
"Quiet" is banned for being vague, gesturing at a quality instead of naming one. "Just" is banned as non-committal filler. A blocklist without reasons produces synonym-swapping. The reason is what produces a rewrite.
Em dashes are reduced, not eliminated
Target of zero to two per post. Eliminating them is its own tell. Most rules that sound like "never do X" should be "do X less than you want to."
Template repetition is the real failure mode
Not bad sentences. Sameness of shape: sensory opening, section breaks, closing open question, every time. The instructions require checking the last two published posts and picking a different entry point. An open question at the end is the safest available ending, which is exactly why it starts to feel hollow.
Show-don't-tell got an operational test
Cover the sentence that states the point. If the surrounding detail still gets the reader there, cut the sentence. The rule also names the two places tells cluster: paragraph openers that announce what the paragraph is about to demonstrate, and the sentence right after a good image that explains what the image meant. Both take the conclusion back from the reader.
Images extend the idea, they don't illustrate it
A post about attention doesn't get a photo of a phone. It might get a person at a window, or hands at rest. Search the emotional register, not the subject matter.

Publishing is now: read the draft, edit it, say approve, run one command. The diff is one file. There's a distribution queue holding roughly six weeks of scheduled material.

What it cost, honestly

The system didn't make writing free. It moved my time from producing drafts to editing them, and I still rewrite parts of every post. That's the trade I wanted, not a shortfall. Editing my own approximate draft is a different and better use of an hour than staring at an empty file. But anyone expecting to press a button and receive a finished essay is going to be disappointed, and should be.

Three things I'd tell myself at the start:

The constraint file can't be written up front

Nearly every rule in it exists because a bad draft taught me it was needed. I tried to anticipate them and produced generic writing advice. The specific rules, the ones that actually change output, are all forensic.

Voice is re-derived, never captured

The instructions tell the agent to read two or three existing posts before writing, every single run. I haven't found a way to encode the voice that beats going and looking at it. That's either a limitation of my approach or a fact about voice.

No dedupe, and it shows

Two drafts of the same essay ended up in the queue under different names, and I didn't catch it until I was scheduling posts. The pipeline has no memory of what it's already tried.

The interesting design problem in working with these tools turned out not to be what they can produce. It's whether you can state what "good" means precisely enough that the judgment survives being handed to something that doesn't have any.

Most of that document, once I finally wrote it, was about what not to do.
