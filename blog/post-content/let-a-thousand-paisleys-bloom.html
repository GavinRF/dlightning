By 1860 the looms in Paisley, Scotland could carry fifteen colors through a shawl. That same year, shawls arriving from Kashmir, four thousand miles away at the northern edge of the Indian subcontinent, carried around sixty. Paisley won anyway. Not the market, which fell apart in the 1870s when fashion moved on. The Scottish town won name recognition on volume.

The teardrop with the bent tip is the boteh, a Persian shape out of what is now Iran. From there it traveled east about fifteen hundred miles and was in Kashmir by the 1400s, woven into shawls there under Akbar, the Mughal emperor who annexed Kashmir in 1586 and ruled most of the subcontinent from Agra. Four hundred years later, nobody walks into a fabric store and asks for boteh. They ask for paisley, after the mill town on the White Cart Water, seven miles outside Glasgow.

A Kashmiri kani shawl is built with small wooden bobbins by hand, and depending on how dense the design gets, a weaver builds up about an inch a day. Months for a simple one. Years for a good one. The Scottish answer to sixty colors was a faster loom. Paisley's upgrades arrived in stages: a loom attachment in 1812 took the count from two colors to five, the Jacquard loom in 1820, and by 1850 more than seven thousand weavers working in one town. Fifteen was the number of colors the machine could hold and still turn out a shawl at a price a clerk's wife in Glasgow could pay.

The reduction didn't stop at the loom either. Roller printing put the pattern onto cloth that it was never woven into at all, one engraved copper cylinder per color, six on a common machine. The price kept falling from there. The shape Akbar's weavers gave a year to is now on thin button-down shirts, upholstery, notebooks, phone cases, on anything with a printable surface. Buyers got the pattern. On a roller it lives in the copper, engraved once and repeated. In Kashmir it lived in people: one drew the design, another wrote it out as a color code, a color master set every shade, and a reader called them out to the weavers at the loom line by line, three blue, four yellow, six red.

Eighty years after the shawl market fell apart, on the other side of the Himalayas from Kashmir, the Chinese Communist Party, under Mao, put out a slogan: let a hundred flowers bloom, let a hundred schools of thought contend. The flowers were opinions. The hundred schools were borrowed from the Warring States period more than two thousand years earlier, when rival philosophies contended for supremacy of ideas and not of territory. With this, citizens were invited to criticize the Party freely.

People did, for five weeks that spring, students and workers and academics, and the criticism ran sharper than anyone at the top had planned for. By that summer the government had answered with the Anti-Rightist Campaign. Roughly four hundred thousand residents were branded rightists and sent to penal camps or into rural labor. Whether it was a trap from the start or became one afterward, the invitation had handed the Party the names of everyone who took it at its word.

Ten years later and half a world away, the boteh shape found the psychedelic era waiting for it. The Beatles carried paisley into the mainstream around the summer of 1967, Bowie and Queen wore it on stage, and in 1968 Fender finished a run of Telecasters by gluing pink paisley wallpaper onto the bodies. Prince named Paisley Park, his recording studio, after it two decades on.

The cost of turning out a pattern, a page, a face, a passable paragraph has fallen close enough to zero that you can ask for a thousand paisleys and have them before you've finished asking.

They still cost something to model, to store, to move, to render. Complexity kept its price and changed its denomination, out of thread and labor and into compute and power.

The power comes off a regional grid and the cooling water out of a local river or aquifer, and the people who live beside the racks are rarely the ones who typed the request. Paisley ran on the same arrangement. A shawl was cheap in Glasgow partly because Kashmir paid the difference.

Then there's the part you settle yourself, in hours at a screen. The thread in a kani shawl was never the expensive part. The year of weaving was. Make the material free and the rest of the bill falls on the part that was already expensive: time.

A phone case can carry all sixty colors now, or six thousand, and it costs what a case with four costs. Every count before that was a constraint, another bobbin or another cylinder to engrave, and each one made somebody work out which colors earned their place. Nobody in the print department is counting anymore.

None of which makes the access less remarkable. A person with a phone can call up more in a second than an emperor could have had carried to him overland.

If you've ever written a customer survey, you've set a loom. How did you hear about us? Search, a friend, an ad, social media. Four boxes, fast to tick and fast to chart. Under them sits a fifth, marked "Other," with a blank line to fill in, there because you knew you'd missed something. It's where everything else went. It's the highest-resolution material a product collects and the one column nobody opens, because it won't aggregate. Ticked boxes make a chart. Four thousand sentences make a chore.

Open it. Read fifty entries end to end, in order. Not summarized, not tagged, not run through a model that hands you back five themes. What you're after is the sentence a person needed to write badly enough to type it into a box they had every reason to assume nobody would ever read. A kernel of truth. Then go find that person and ask what they meant.

Fifty takes about twenty minutes, and the twenty minutes is the first reason the column stays shut. The material got free and the reading didn't.

The 1956 hundred flowers invitation was a survey too, and issuing it cost nothing. Millions of letters reached the Premier's Office in a single month, and the whole price landed on the people who wrote them. Your research is not that. The asking is still the free half of it.

The sentences in that column are the other forty-five colors. The reduced version is going to get the most attention regardless, and that part isn't worth fighting, because fifteen colors and a Jacquard loom put a shawl on a clerk's wife and an inch a day never would have. What's worth fighting is what comes after, when nobody left in the building can say what got dropped to reach fifteen. The museum in the town of Paisley holds around twelve hundred Paisley shawls, the largest such collection anywhere, along with the Kashmir originals those looms spent seventy years failing to match.

Your version of that archive is a spreadsheet column, and it's already full: four thousand people describing the thing in their own colors, none of it cut down to fit a box. The thousand paisleys don't need generating. Most of them are already there.
