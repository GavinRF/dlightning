Let a Thousand Paisleys Bloom

When creation costs nothing the whole bill lands on the hours.

Dlightning  
September 9, 2026
Sparks list

By 1860 the looms in Paisley, Scotland could carry fifteen colors through a shawl. That same year, shawls arriving from Kashmir, four thousand miles away at the northern edge of the Indian subcontinent, carried around sixty. Paisley won anyway. Not the market, which fell apart in the 1870s when fashion moved on. The Scottish town won the name on volume.

The teardrop with the bent tip is the boteh, a Persian shape out of what is now Iran. From there it traveled east about fifteen hundred miles and was in Kashmir by the 1400s, woven into shawls there under Akbar, the Mughal emperor who annexed Kashmir in 1586 and ruled most of the subcontinent from Agra. Four hundred years later, nobody walks into a fabric store and asks for boteh. They ask for paisley, after the mill town on the White Cart Water, seven miles outside Glasgow.

Scottish manufacturers dropped colors for throughput. A Kashmiri kani shawl is built with small wooden bobbins by hand, and depending on how dense the design gets, a weaver builds up about an inch a day. Months for a simple one. Years for a good one. Scottish paisley upgrades arrived in stages: a loom attachment in 1812 took the count from two colors to five, the Jacquard loom in 1820, and by 1850 more than seven thousand weavers working in one town. Fifteen was the number of colors the machine could hold and still turn out a shawl at a price a clerk's wife in Glasgow could pay.

The reduction didn't stop at the loom either. Roller printing put the pattern onto cloth that it was never woven into at all, one engraved copper cylinder per color, six on a common machine. The price kept falling from there. The shape Akbar's weavers gave a year to is now on thin button-down shirts, upholstery, notebooks, phone cases, on anything with a surface. Buyers got the pattern. What went missing was whatever the other forty-five colors had been doing.

In 1956 the Chinese Communist Party, under Mao, put out a slogan: let a hundred flowers bloom, let a hundred schools of thought contend. The flowers were opinions. The hundred schools were borrowed from the Warring States period more than two thousand years earlier, when rival philosophies contended for supremacy of ideas and not of territory. With this, citizens were invited to criticize the Party freely.

People did, for five weeks that spring, students and workers and academics, and the criticism ran sharper than anyone at the top had planned for. By that summer the government had answered with the Anti-Rightist Campaign. Roughly four hundred thousand residents were branded rightists and sent to penal camps or into rural labor. The hundred flowers slogan had worked as a census.

The boteh shape found the psychedelic era waiting for it. The Beatles carried paisley into the mainstream around the summer of 1967, Bowie and Queen wore it on stage, and in 1968 Fender finished a run of Telecasters by gluing pink paisley wallpaper onto the bodies. Prince named Paisley Park, his recording studio, after it two decades on.

Terence McKenna, an ethnobotanist who lectured through the 1980s and 90s on psychedelic plants and the origins of consciousness, bent the Chinese slogan into Let a thousand paisleys bloom in a talk called The World Could Be Anything. We had learned to build everything out of interchangeable parts, starting with the identical letters that come off a printing press, and had grown rich in things while getting duller in mind and more alike in spirit. What Terence wanted was the world lit back up and various again, with the cost of the materials no longer deciding what anything got to look like.

Terence wasn't talking about fabric. He meant something more abstract and more complex, like art or language, and he was clear about why we mostly don't in the material world. Ornament costs money. Plain surfaces are cheap, which is most of the reason we find them everywhere in modern life. Start adding filigree and the price climbs.

He blamed the Bauhaus. Walter Gropius opened the school in 1919 on a craft manifesto, calling for a guild of craftsmen with the barrier between craftsman and artist torn down. By 1923 Gropius wanted an architecture fit for machines, radios and fast cars, and a flat surface is what a machine repeats cheaply. The plainness Terence objected to had a manufacturing case sitting under the aesthetic one, and the school carrying the blame for it had started out arguing for the hand. For all that, its most profitable product was wallpaper.

That constraint produced a chair Marcel Breuer bent out of steel tube in 1925, after buying a bicycle and noticing how little its frame weighed. The chair is still being made a hundred years on, and copied worldwide under other names. Breuer's chair and a shawl at sixty colors carry the same evidence: someone with taste working inside a limit. Taste never got cheaper.

The fix was to go somewhere the material doesn't charge you. In a space made of light. The palace of Versailles costs the same to build as a hamburger stand, because on a disk the two of them are only programs. Drop the price of making to nothing and uniformity loses its excuse.

The cost of turning out a pattern, a page, a face, a passable paragraph has fallen close enough to zero that you can ask for a thousand paisleys and have them before you've finished asking.

Versailles still takes up more disk than a hamburger stand. More to model, more to store, more to move, more to render. Complexity kept its price and changed its denomination, out of stone and labor and into compute and power.

Somebody pays for the electricity and for the water that cools the racks, and they generally don't live where the request was typed. Then there's the part you settle yourself, in hours at a screen.

Better costs more, and most of what it costs was never material. The thread in a kani shawl was never the expensive part. The year of weaving was. Make the material free and the whole bill lands on the part that was already expensive: time.

A phone case can carry all sixty colors now, or six thousand, and it costs what a case with four costs. Inkjet ended the arithmetic. Every count before it was a constraint: the next color meant another bobbin, another loom, another cylinder to engrave and hold in register. Each of those numbers made somebody work out which colors earned their place. A designer's file carries a number, but nobody in the print department is counting.

None of which makes the access less remarkable. A person with a phone can call up more in a second than an emperor could have had carried to him overland.

The same trade is sitting in most research anybody runs. A dropdown is the loom setting, fast to fill and fast to count, with somebody deciding in advance which answers are available. The free-text box at the bottom is where the rest went, the one labeled "Other," or "Anything else we should know?", the field that exists because whoever wrote the questions knew the options were incomplete and needed somewhere to put the overflow. It's the highest-resolution material any product collects and the one column nobody opens, because it won't aggregate. A dropdown selection makes a chart. Four thousand sentences make a chore.

Open it. Read fifty entries end to end, in order. Not summarized, not tagged, not run through a model that hands you back five themes. What you're after is the sentence a person needed to write badly enough to type it into a box they had every reason to assume nobody would ever read. A kernel of truth. Then go find that person and ask what they meant.

Fifty takes about twenty minutes, and the twenty minutes is the first reason the column stays shut. The material got free and the reading didn't.

The 1956 hundred flowers invitation was a survey too, and issuing it cost nothing. Millions of letters reached the Premier's Office in a single month, and the whole price landed on the people who wrote them. Your research is not that. The asking is still the free half of it.

Those are the other forty-five colors. The reduced version is going to get the most attention regardless, and that part isn't worth fighting, because fifteen colors and a Jacquard loom put a shawl on a clerk's wife and an inch a day never would have. What's worth fighting is what comes after, when nobody left in the building can say what got dropped to reach fifteen. The museum in the town of Paisley holds around twelve hundred Paisley shawls, the largest such collection anywhere, along with the Kashmir originals those looms spent seventy years failing to match.

Your version of that archive is a spreadsheet column, and it's already full. The thousand paisleys don't need generating. Most of them are already there.

Reading those columns, and running down what the sentences point at, is work we do for clients. No judgment, no labeling, only insights.
