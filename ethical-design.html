The EthicalDesign Pyramid

Deciding what is good or bad for other people is complicated. The platinum rule says to treat others as they would like to be treated, and the only way to know that is to ask.

Each layer carries the one above it. A product only reaches delight once everything beneath it is solid.

Layer one · The base

Human Rights

The rights a product has to respect before anything can be built on top of it. If this layer is weak, the layers above have nothing to stand on.

Decentralized, Private, Open, Interoperable, Accessible, Secure, & Sustainable

What the base is made of

Ask

Is the power to make change in the hands of a few powerful individuals, or many equal partners?

When a few people can change the rules, everyone else depends on their goodwill. Spread the power to make decisions across the people who build and use the product, and give them a real say in what it becomes.

Ask

Will sensitive user data be safe? Do users have the ability to choose when and with whom they share their personal information?

Collect only what the product needs, say plainly what happens to it, and let people change their minds later. Sharing should be a choice someone makes, not a default they have to find and undo.

Ask

If someone tried to break in tomorrow, what would they reach, and who would pay for it?

Privacy depends on security. A promise to keep data safe means little if the system holding it is easy to break into. Protect accounts, encrypt what is stored and sent, and plan for the day something goes wrong. Security is one of our design principles too.

Ask

Is this platform exclusionary, or available to all?

Open standards let people take their work and their data somewhere else. Interoperability lets a product work alongside the tools people already use. Accessibility means people of every ability, on older devices and slow connections, can use it as well as anyone.

Ask
As a business

Are you providing a service people rely on? Will it be around a month from now, three years from now, three decades from now? Is there a backup plan for when the time comes to shut down?

People build routines, and sometimes livelihoods, on the services they use. Plan for the long run, and if the service does have to close, give people warning, time and a way to take what is theirs.

For the planet

Does this product rely heavily on natural resources without remediation?

Servers, devices and shipping all draw on energy and materials. Count what the product uses, cut what it doesn’t need, and put back what it takes.

Layer two · The middle

Human Effort

Once a product respects people, it has to work for them, without disruption or frustration.

Functional, Convenient, & Reliable

Functional, Convenient, & Reliable

Ask

Does it do what it sets out to do, without disruption or frustration, every time someone needs it?

Product details should hold to design principles that protect customers from disruption and frustration. Our approach starts by understanding what the product sets out to achieve, so we can work out how to build it.

To keep problems from reaching the people who use it

  1. Evaluate needs

    Find out what customers need from the product, from the customers themselves, before deciding what it does.

  2. Plan explicitly

    Write down how the product will be made, in enough detail that gaps show up on paper instead of in use.

  3. Test rigorously

    Test every feature and function under the conditions people will really use it in, not only the ones it was built for.

Layer three · the top

Human Experience

Delightful

Delightful

Ask

Does interacting with the product elicit positive emotions?

This is the pinnacle of product achievement, and it can only be reached when the two layers beneath it have been met. People want to feel good and be happy, and products should aspire to deliver joy. A product that charms people while it leaks their data, or through a checkout that fails half the time, has not reached the top.

Delight, measured honestly

A healthy amount

Online relationships are increasingly parasocial: one-way engagement that rarely supports real interaction, and deepens the public epidemic of loneliness.

There are benefits to using technology, and there are risks. A product can be enjoyable and still take more of someone’s time and attention than they would choose to give it.

Ask

How much time do people spend with this product, and what is a healthy amount of engagement?

Delight, done right

  1. Show people their usage

    Give people information about how they use the product, so they can decide how much time to spend with it and when to shift their focus elsewhere.

  2. Design for positive activities

    Build around things that leave people better off, such as learning something new or connecting with the people they love.

  3. Notice how people feel

    Find out whether people feel anxious, depressed or stressed while using the product, and treat it as a defect to fix.

  4. Leave them in charge

    Give people the tools to feel good and improve their own experience, so they decide how the product fits into their life.

As information gets quicker and easier to deliver and access, technology can pull people further from their own lives.
